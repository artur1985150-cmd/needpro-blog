In short

  • Compensation under Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 is €250, €400 or €600, set by flight distance, not by ticket price.
  • A delay counts on arrival at the final destination: 3 hours or more, unless the cause was an extraordinary circumstance.
  • Every flight from an EU airport is covered; flights into the EU only if the airline is an EU carrier.
  • Claim from the operating airline first; after 2 months without a fair answer, go to the national enforcement body of the country where it happened.
  • Regulation (EU) 2026/2202 amends the rules from 23 October 2027 and keeps the same amounts; until then the 2004 table applies.

EU261 pays a fixed sum by distance: €250 for flights up to 1,500 km, €400 for flights within the EU over 1,500 km and other flights of 1,500 to 3,500 km, and €600 for the rest. You get it when you arrive 3 or more hours late, when the airline cancels late or denies you boarding, unless an extraordinary circumstance caused it. Rules checked on 30 October 2026.

A €40 ticket and a €900 ticket earn the same sum, because Article 7 sets it by distance alone. This page has the full table as the regulation and the European Commission write it, the cases that cut it in half, which flights are covered at all, and where to complain when the airline says no.

The EU261 compensation table

Flight distance Delay on arrival Compensation
1,500 km or less 3 hours or more €250
Over 1,500 km within the EU 3 hours or more €400
Other flights of 1,500 to 3,500 km 3 hours or more €400
Over 3,500 km, from the EU to a non-EU country or into the EU on an EU airline 3 hours to under 4 €300
Over 3,500 km, same flights 4 hours or more €600

Sources: Article 7(1) of Regulation (EC) No 261/2004 and the "delay at arrival" table on the European Commission's Your Europe page, both checked 30 October 2026.

The same three amounts (€250, €400, €600) apply to a cancellation without enough notice and to denied boarding against your will.

How the distance is measured: Article 7(4) says by the great circle route, and Article 7(1) says to the last destination where you arrive late. With a connection booked on one reservation, that is your final destination, not the first stop.

When the airline may pay half

Article 7(2) lets the airline cut compensation by 50% when it re-routes you and you reach your final destination not much later than planned:

Distance Arrival no later than Reduced amount
1,500 km or less 2 hours after schedule €125
Over 1,500 km within the EU, or 1,500 to 3,500 km 3 hours after schedule €200
Longer flights 4 hours after schedule €300

Compensation is paid in cash, by bank transfer or by cheque. Vouchers are allowed only with your signed agreement (Article 7(3)).

Which flights does EU261 cover?

Article 3 and the Your Europe page set the scope:

  • Any flight departing from an airport in the EU, on any airline.
  • A flight arriving in the EU from outside, only if the operating airline is an EU carrier.
  • EU here means the 27 member states, including the Azores, Madeira, the Canary Islands and the French outermost regions, but not the Faroe Islands. Your Europe says the rules also apply to flights to and from Iceland, Norway and Switzerland.

The inbound rule is easy to miss. Citizens Information gives an example: New York to Dublin on Aer Lingus is covered; the same route on American Airlines is not, because that airline is not licensed in Europe.

You also need a confirmed booking and to check in on time: as the airline told you in writing, or at least 45 minutes before the published departure if it gave no time. Free tickets and discounted fares not available to the public are excluded, but frequent flyer tickets are covered (Article 3(3)). You also lose the claim if you already received compensation or assistance for the same problem under the law of a non-EU country.

Delays, cancellations and denied boarding

Delay. Article 6 of the regulation deals with care while you wait. For money, Your Europe states the rule plainly: arrive at your final destination 3 hours or more late and you are entitled to compensation, unless the delay was due to extraordinary circumstances. The airline must prove those, for example with logbook extracts or incident reports.

Cancellation. Article 5(1)(c) removes compensation in three cases:

  1. You were told at least two weeks before departure.
  2. You were told between two weeks and seven days before, and offered a flight leaving no more than 2 hours earlier and arriving less than 4 hours later.
  3. You were told less than seven days before, and offered a flight leaving no more than 1 hour earlier and arriving less than 2 hours later.

Proving when you were told is the airline's job (Article 5(4)).

Denied boarding. If you checked in on time and the airline refuses to carry you against your will, for example because of overbooking, it owes compensation immediately (Article 4(3)). Your Europe names the exceptions: reasonable grounds such as health, safety or security, or missing travel documents.

Missed connection. With all flights on one reservation, arriving more than 3 hours late at the final destination counts, according to Your Europe. Missing the connection because of a slow security queue or arriving late to the gate does not.

What counts as an extraordinary circumstance?

Your Europe gives examples that do count: air traffic management decisions, political instability, adverse weather and security risks. It also lists what does not:

  • most technical problems found during maintenance or caused by failure to maintain the aircraft;
  • a collision of mobile boarding stairs with the aircraft;
  • strikes by the airline's own staff.

Germany's Luftfahrt-Bundesamt says the same about technical faults and points to the EU Court of Justice ruling of 22 December 2008, C-549/07 (Wallentin-Hermann v Alitalia). A strike outside the airline may count, but the airline must show the link to your delay and that it could not have been avoided with all reasonable measures.

Care, refunds and rerouting: owed even without compensation

An extraordinary circumstance cancels the money, not the care. Articles 6 and 9 require the airline to give you meals and refreshments in reasonable relation to the wait, and two phone calls or emails, once the delay at departure reaches:

Distance Care starts after
1,500 km or less 2 hours
Over 1,500 km within the EU, or 1,500 to 3,500 km 3 hours
Longer flights 4 hours

If the new departure is the next day or later, add a hotel and transport to it. If the delay reaches 5 hours, you can give up the trip and take a refund within seven days (Articles 6 and 8). After a cancellation or denied boarding you choose between a refund, rerouting at the earliest opportunity or rerouting at a later date that suits you.

If the airline gives you nothing, Your Europe says to buy what you reasonably need, keep every receipt and claim the costs back.

If you are packing a power bank for the rerouted flight, check the power bank rules for China when your route passes through a Chinese airport.

Driving instead of flying? Swiss motorways need a CHF 40 vignette, and French low-emission zones need a Crit'Air sticker.

How to claim EU261 compensation, step by step

  1. Write to the airline that operated the flight, using its complaint form. Your Europe stresses that only the operating airline is responsible, even if you bought the ticket elsewhere. Outbound and return flights count as two separate flights.
  2. Keep your booking, boarding pass, any delay notice and receipts.
  3. If the airline does not answer within 2 months, or you are not satisfied, complain to the national enforcement body of the country where the incident happened. It gives a non-binding opinion.
  4. You can also use an alternative dispute resolution body. Your Europe notes this is open only to EU residents.
  5. As a last step, go to court, for example through the European Small Claims procedure. Time limits for court claims are set by each country's national law.

Where to complain if the airline refuses: Ireland and Germany

Which body handles your complaint depends on where the flight left from. Citizens Information explains it with Dublin to Malaga: a problem on the outbound leg goes to the Irish Aviation Authority, on the return leg to the Spanish body, AESA.

Ireland. The Irish Aviation Authority (IAA) is the national enforcement body. You register on its online platform, MySRS, and file the complaint form there. Older guides that point to the Commission for Aviation Regulation are out of date: that office was merged into the IAA in 2023.

Germany. The Luftfahrt-Bundesamt (LBA) is the national enforcement and complaints body, but its own FAQ adds a limit worth knowing before you write to it: the LBA supervises whether airlines follow the rules and does not enforce your civil claim for money. To get paid, you go to court, or to a conciliation body, which is free for passengers. The FAQ names söp (Schlichtungsstelle für den öffentlichen Personenverkehr) for airlines that belong to it, and the official conciliation body at the Federal Office of Justice for airlines that do not. It also mentions an online claim service of the Federal Ministry of Justice, still in a trial phase.

For other countries, Your Europe links to the list of national authorities.

The 2026 revision: does it change the table yet?

Not yet. On 13 July 2026 the Council of the EU gave final clearance to a regulation amending Regulation 261/2004. It was published in the Official Journal on 2 October 2026 as Regulation (EU) 2026/2202, and its Article 3 says it applies from 23 October 2027. The new text keeps €250, €400 and €600 and the right to compensation when arrival is more than three hours late. Until 23 October 2027 the 2004 table above is the one that applies.

Questions people ask

How much is EU261 compensation?

€250 for flights of 1,500 km or less, €400 for flights within the EU over 1,500 km and other flights of 1,500 to 3,500 km, and €600 for longer flights. For a long-haul flight that arrives 3 to 4 hours late, the European Commission's table gives €300.

How long does a flight have to be delayed for EU261 compensation?

3 hours or more at your final destination. The clock runs on arrival, not departure, according to the European Commission's Your Europe page.

Does EU261 cover a flight from outside the EU on a non-EU airline?

No. Flying into the EU, the rules apply only if the operating airline is an EU carrier. Citizens Information's example: New York to Dublin on Aer Lingus is covered, on American Airlines it is not.

Can the airline pay me in vouchers?

Only if you agree in writing. Article 7(3) says compensation is paid in cash, by bank transfer or cheque, or in vouchers with the passenger's signed agreement.

Is a technical fault an extraordinary circumstance?

Usually not. Your Europe lists most technical problems found during maintenance, and strikes by the airline's own staff, as not extraordinary. Germany's LBA cites the EU Court of Justice ruling C-549/07 on the same point.

Do I need a claims company?

No. You can claim from the airline yourself, then go to the national enforcement body, an alternative dispute body or a court. Claims agencies are optional.
