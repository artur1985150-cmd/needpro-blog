In short

  • Most of Europe, the EU and the UK included, is not on CDC's high-risk list, so a dog flying from there to the US needs only the free CDC Dog Import Form receipt.
  • The dog must be at least 6 months old, carry a microchip a universal scanner can read, and look healthy on arrival.
  • Belarus, Moldova, Russia, Turkey and Ukraine are on CDC's high-risk list (checked 31 October 2026). A dog that was in one of them in the past 6 months needs a rabies vaccination, more documents and a reservation at a CDC-registered animal care facility.
  • A layover counts only if the dog clears customs there. Airlines must deny boarding to a dog without a receipt, but you can fill in the form on your phone at the airport.
  • Cabin or hold is the airline's decision. IATA says only small dogs and cats go in the cabin, and the crate must let the dog stand, sit, turn around and lie down.

Flying a dog from Europe to the US usually takes one free online document: the CDC Dog Import Form receipt. On 31 October 2026, CDC treated the EU, the UK and most of Europe as low-risk for dog rabies, so the dog only had to be at least 6 months old, microchipped and healthy on arrival. Dogs that spent time in Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Russia or Turkey in the past 6 months face much stricter rules.

Which European countries does CDC count as high-risk?

CDC's list of high-risk countries for dog rabies, updated 15 April 2026, names five European countries: Belarus, Moldova, Russia, Turkey and Ukraine. Every other European country is off the list, and CDC says that a country not on the list is not considered high risk. That covers all EU member states, the UK, Norway and Switzerland.

A few more names on the list matter for trips that start in Europe:

  • Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia and Kazakhstan;
  • Ceuta and Melilla, the Spanish cities in North Africa (mainland Spain is not listed);
  • Turkey, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, where many flights from Europe to the US connect.

What counts is where the dog has been in the 6 months before it arrives in the US, not its passport or where it was born. A German dog that spent a summer in Ukraine is treated as a high-risk dog until 6 months have passed.

What a dog from the EU or UK needs to enter the US

Three conditions and one document. CDC's page for dogs from rabies-free and low-risk countries asks that the dog is at least 6 months old on the day it arrives, has a microchip a universal scanner can detect, and looks healthy. The CDC Dog Import Form receipt is the only document CDC requires.

CDC does not ask these dogs for a rabies certificate or a titer test, though it strongly recommends rabies vaccination for all dogs. They can arrive at any US airport, seaport or land border.

For a dog vaccinated outside the US that has been in a high-risk country, CDC's foreign-vaccinated page adds a titer test from a CDC-approved lab (without one, 28 days' quarantine at the facility), a form endorsed by an official government vet and arrival at the airport where the animal care facility is. If that is your case, read CDC's page for it in full before you book.

How to fill in the CDC Dog Import Form

You fill it in online on CDC's site, one form per dog. The person bringing the dog does it, and CDC's instructions say the receipt arrives by email within 15 minutes after you submit and confirm your email address. CDC recommends doing it a few days, or up to six months, before you travel.

The form asks for:

  • your date of birth and a passport, driver's license or air waybill number;
  • your email address and phone number;
  • the dog's name, age, sex, breed, color and markings;
  • the purpose of the trip, how the dog travels, the country it departs from and the arrival date.

On 5 February 2026 CDC changed the look of the receipt. Its instructions say the import rules did not change and that receipts from the old version still work if the details are right and they have not expired. Keep the receipt on your phone and print a copy. You show it to the airline and to US Customs and Border Protection.

What if you connect in Istanbul, Doha or Dubai?

A layover in a high-risk country counts only if your dog clears customs there. CDC's layover page says a dog is considered to have entered a country if it clears customs, and then it must meet the high-risk rules. A connection where the dog never clears customs does not make it a high-risk dog.

Turkey, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates are all on CDC's high-risk list. CDC's page gives no separate rule for dogs that stay airside, and tells owners who are unsure whether the dog will enter the country to check with the airline. A direct flight from an EU or UK airport avoids the question.

What the airline checks, and cabin or hold

The airline checks the CDC receipt before boarding. CDC's page for air carriers, updated 7 April 2026, tells airlines to deny boarding to a dog with missing documents, a dog that looks ill and a dog under 6 months. If you forgot the form, CDC says you can complete it on your phone before boarding.

Whether the dog flies in the cabin or the hold is the airline's call, not CDC's. Most airlines follow IATA's Live Animals Regulations, and IATA's page for pet owners says:

  • only small dogs and cats can travel in the cabin, and some airlines carry all pets as special baggage in a heated, ventilated hold;
  • the crate must let the dog turn around while standing, stand and sit upright, and lie down in a natural position;
  • snub-nosed breeds need a crate 10% larger, and IATA advises against flying boxers, pugs, bulldogs and Pekingese in the hot season;
  • most airlines want a health certificate for any animal they carry;
  • you should reconfirm the pet's booking at least 48 hours before departure.

IATA advises against sedating dogs for the flight. CDC's carrier page also tells airlines to generate an air waybill for each dog, including dogs in the cabin, unless the airline has a CDC waiver. If entry is refused in the US, the airline has to fly the dog back to the departure country within 72 hours. Ask your airline which papers it checks at the desk, because some ask for more than CDC.

Bringing your US dog back from Europe

A US dog coming home from a holiday in France, Italy or the UK follows the same low-risk rules as any European dog: the receipt, 6 months of age, a microchip and good health. CDC's rules apply to a dog "at time of entry or return to the U.S.", so American owners fill in the form too.

The harder case is a US dog that visited Ukraine, Turkey or another high-risk country. CDC's page for US-vaccinated dogs asks for a Certification of U.S.-Issued Rabies Vaccination, completed by a USDA-accredited vet before the dog leaves the US and endorsed by USDA. That form has to exist before departure, so plan it before the trip, not in Europe.

Does USDA also check dogs from Europe?

Only in narrow cases. The USDA's animal health service (APHIS) says CDC is the primary authority for dog imports. APHIS adds its own rules only for dogs from countries affected by foot-and-mouth disease or screwworm, for dogs that handle livestock, and for dogs imported for sale or adoption, which need an APHIS permit.

If your dog is a pet travelling with you, open APHIS's page for pet dogs and its country disease status check before the flight. Your destination state can have rules too, and APHIS links to the state entry requirements.

The flight back to Europe later

The EU has its own rules, so plan the return leg before you leave. Your Europe says a dog entering from outside the EU needs a microchip before the rabies shot, a first shot at 12 weeks or older, a 21-day wait, and an EU animal health certificate from an official vet issued at most 10 days before arrival.

Most non-EU countries also need a titer test, with some exceptions. An EU resident can skip the 3-month wait after the blood test when coming back from a short stay outside the EU, if the dog was vaccinated and tested before leaving and the vet recorded it in the EU pet passport. Check the EU's page for whether the US needs the test.

A checklist for the flight from Europe

Six steps, in this order, cover a typical dog flying from an EU or UK airport to the US. Start at least a few days before the flight, and earlier if the dog has been anywhere near the high-risk list.

  1. List every country the dog has been in during the last 6 months and compare it with CDC's high-risk list.
  2. Make sure the dog will be at least 6 months old on the arrival date and that its microchip reads on a universal scanner.
  3. Fill in the CDC Dog Import Form a few days before the flight and save the receipt.
  4. Choose a route that does not make the dog clear customs in a high-risk country.
  5. Ask the airline about cabin or hold, crate size and its health certificate, and reconfirm the booking 48 hours before departure.
  6. If the dog will fly back to Europe, read the EU rules now.

Your own trip has paperwork too: check how long your passport must be valid and, if you travel on the Visa Waiver Program, how long an ESTA lasts. Moving somewhere other than the US? CDC and US Customs and Border Protection decide at the border, and the airline decides at the gate. Read CDC's current pages before you fly. Questions people ask

Do I need a rabies titer test to fly my dog from Europe to the US?

Not if the dog has only been in countries that are not on CDC's high-risk list for the past 6 months. For those dogs the CDC Dog Import Form receipt is the only document CDC requires. CDC still recommends rabies vaccination for all dogs, and dogs from high-risk countries such as Ukraine or Turkey face titer and vaccination rules.

Is the CDC Dog Import Form free?

Yes. CDC's pages call the form free. You fill it in online, confirm your email address, and the receipt usually arrives by email within 15 minutes.

Can my puppy fly from Europe to the US?

Only once it is at least 6 months old on the day it arrives. CDC applies the same age rule to every dog entering or returning to the US, and airlines are told to deny boarding to dogs under 6 months.

Does my dog need a new form for every trip?

For a dog from a low-risk country the receipt is valid for 6 months and several entries from the same country. Fly in from a different country, or visit a high-risk country, and you fill in a new form. Receipts for high-risk dogs cover one entry only.

Which airline lets my dog fly in the cabin to the US?

That depends on the airline, not on CDC. IATA's guidance says only small dogs and cats can travel in the cabin and some airlines carry all pets in a heated, ventilated hold instead. Ask your airline about cabin space, weight limits and crate size before you book.
