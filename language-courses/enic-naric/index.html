In short

  • ENIC-NARIC is a network of national information centres on recognising qualifications, covering 56 countries on 30 October 2026.
  • ENIC is the wider Council of Europe and UNESCO network; NARIC is the EU's. Most countries have one centre that belongs to both.
  • The centres inform and refer. For study, the university you apply to usually decides; for a regulated profession, a competent authority named by the state does.
  • Under the Lisbon Recognition Convention the body assessing your degree must show a substantial difference before it refuses. You do not have to prove your degree is good enough.
  • Some centres issue paid statements of comparability, others only give guidance. Check the centre's own site before you pay anyone.

ENIC-NARIC is a network of national information centres that tell you how a foreign degree is recognised in their country. On 30 October 2026 it covered 56 countries and worked under the Lisbon Recognition Convention of 1997. The centres inform and refer. The decision usually sits with the university you apply to or, for a regulated profession, with an authority the state has named.

What ENIC-NARIC is

The network's own home page describes it as a collaboration between "the national information centres on academic recognition of qualifications of in total 56 countries". Its joint website, enic-naric.net, is an initiative of the European Commission, the Council of Europe and UNESCO, and it is hosted by CIMEA, the Italian centre.

The joint site does not assess anything. It is a directory: for each member country it points you to the national centre and to the bodies that actually take decisions there. The people it says it serves are individuals planning to study or work abroad, credential evaluators, universities and employers.

So when someone tells you to "get your degree checked by ENIC-NARIC", what they mean in practice is the national centre of the country you are moving to.

Is ENIC the same as NARIC?

They are two networks that work as one:

Name What it stands for Run with
ENIC European Network of Information Centres Council of Europe and UNESCO
NARIC National Academic Recognition Information Centres in the European Union European Commission

Source: enic-naric.net, checked 30 October 2026.

ENIC reaches beyond the EU, because the Lisbon Convention does: the ENIC-NARIC page on the Convention says it has been "signed and ratified by 55 countries". NARIC covers the EU side. In most EU countries one office carries both labels, which is why you see "ENIC-NARIC Germany" or "ENIC-NARIC Ireland" written as a single name.

Wikipedia-style pages still quote older member counts. The network's own page is the one to trust, and it said 56 when we read it.

What a centre will and will not do for you

A centre gives information about recognition in its country and tells you which body decides in your case. NeedPro's checked fact on the network adds one more point: some centres also issue paid statements of comparability, while others only give guidance and refer you on.

What a centre usually does not do is take the final decision for you. That depends on why you need recognition:

  • You want to study, or to use an academic title: academic recognition, decided normally by the university.
  • You want to work in a regulated profession: professional recognition, decided by a competent authority.
  • You want to work in a profession that is not regulated: no formal recognition is needed, and the employer judges your qualifications.

The next two sections take these one at a time.

One warning from the network itself: its home page says "some third parties appear to be falsely presenting themselves as ENIC/NARIC centres". Reach your national centre through the country list on enic-naric.net, not through an ad or a search result, before you pay for any statement.

Who decides when you want to study

The ENIC-NARIC page on academic recognition says: "Academic recognition is normally done by the higher education institutions" when a person wants to begin or continue studies, or use an academic title, after studying abroad.

That means the admitting university, not a central agency, usually decides whether your bachelor's degree opens its master's programme. The same page says universities are asked to provide "clear and transparent information about their procedures and criteria". Before you apply, ask the admissions office for those written criteria. Some countries also have a separate state procedure, such as nostrification; there a different authority decides.

Once you are admitted, other rules take over. If you are heading to Canada, for example, the study permit sets how much you may work: see how many hours international students can work in Canada.

Who decides when you want to work

Here the question is whether the profession is regulated. The network's page on professional recognition says a profession is regulated "if the national legislation of the host country stipulates that specific requirements" have to be met to practise it. This often covers health, safety and social care jobs.

For a regulated profession, the state "nominates competent authorities for the recognition of foreign qualifications", and you apply to that authority. In the EU, the rules for these professions come from Directive 2005/36/EC, and the page points to the EU's Regulated Professions Database to check whether a job is regulated in a given country.

If the profession is not regulated, there is no legal requirement for a licence, certificate or registration. The employer decides whether your education and experience fit the job.

The same degree can need a licence in one country and nothing in the next. A recognised diploma also does not replace a work permit: in Türkiye the employer has to obtain one before you start (see how a Turkish work permit is applied for), and China asks for authenticated degree certificates in the work permit file (see what China's work permit rules require).

Your rights under the Lisbon Convention

The Convention on the Recognition of Qualifications concerning Higher Education in the European Region was signed in Lisbon on 11 April 1997. Two of its rules change how you should read a refusal.

First, recognition is the default. Under Article VI.1, each party recognises higher education qualifications from another party "unless a substantial difference can be shown". A different education system, or course titles that do not match, are not a substantial difference on their own. The assessment is about what you learned and can do.

Second, the burden sits with the assessor. The Convention says: "The responsibility to demonstrate that an application does not fulfil the relevant requirements lies with the body undertaking the assessment." NeedPro's checked fact draws the practical consequence: a refusal has to name the substantial difference it rests on, and you can ask for that reasoning in writing and appeal it.

You still have to do your part. The applicant supplies the documents asked for, in good faith and on time. And the Convention applies only between the states that have ratified it, and only to recognition for study and academic titles. Regulated professions follow their own rules.

Documents that make the assessment easier

The Diploma Supplement is the one most people have never asked for. Europass describes it as a document that "provides information that makes it easier for employers and education institutions to understand your qualification": the type and level of the qualification, the institution, the content of the course and results, and details of the national education system.

You request it from the university where you studied. Europass says graduates in more than 40 countries have the right to get it automatically, free of charge, and in a major European language. Older degrees may need a request after the fact, and NeedPro's fact notes that some institutions charge or cannot produce one for degrees awarded before the Bologna reforms.

Steps before you apply

  1. Decide your purpose: study, a regulated profession or a non-regulated job.
  2. Open enic-naric.net, find the country you are moving to and go to its national centre from there.
  3. For study, write to the university's admissions office and ask for its written criteria for foreign degrees.
  4. For a regulated profession, find the competent authority for that profession; in the EU, check the Regulated Professions Database.
  5. Ask your university for a Diploma Supplement and an official transcript.
  6. If you are refused, ask for the reasons in writing and for the appeal route.

The rules and fees are set by each country, and the decision belongs to the university or authority named there. Confirm the procedure on the national centre's own site. Questions people ask

Is ENIC and NARIC the same?

Not quite. ENIC is the European Network of Information Centres, run with the Council of Europe and UNESCO. NARIC stands for the National Academic Recognition Information Centres in the European Union. They work as one network with one joint website, enic-naric.net, and many countries have a single centre that is both.

What is an ENIC-NARIC certificate?

There is no single network certificate. The joint site only points you to the national centres. Some national centres issue a statement of comparability for a fee; others only advise and send you to the body that decides. What a statement is worth depends on who you show it to.

Does ENIC-NARIC recognition let me work as a doctor or engineer?

Not by itself. If the profession is regulated in the country, you apply to the competent authority the state has named for that profession. Academic recognition of your diploma does not give you the right to practise.

Who decides whether my degree counts for a master's programme abroad?

Normally the university you apply to. The ENIC-NARIC page on academic recognition says this is usually done by the higher education institution, which is asked to publish its procedures and criteria.

Can a centre or university refuse my degree because the system is different?

Under the Lisbon Recognition Convention, a party recognises qualifications from another party unless a substantial difference can be shown, and the body doing the assessment carries the burden of showing it. A different system or different course titles alone are not that.
