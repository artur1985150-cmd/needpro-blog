In short

  • On any day, count back 180 days including that day. The days you spent in the Schengen area in that stretch must add up to 90 or fewer.
  • The day you arrive and the day you leave both count as full days, even if you were there for an hour.
  • The count covers all 29 Schengen countries together. Days in Ireland, Cyprus and the UK do not count.
  • Days on a long-stay (D) visa or a residence permit are not counted. A visa sticker can allow fewer than 90 days, and then the sticker wins.
  • The calculator is an estimate. The border guard decides how long you may stay.

    How the 90/180 rule works

    Article 6(1) of the Schengen Borders Code allows a stay of "no more than 90 days in any 180-day period, which entails considering the 180-day period preceding each day of stay". In plain terms the window moves. It is not a calendar half-year and it does not start on your first entry. Every day you are in Schengen, a border guard can take that day, look back over it and the 179 days before it, and count.

    Three things follow from the text:

    • Article 6(2) makes the day of entry the first day of stay and the day of exit the last. Land at 23:50 and leave at 00:10, and that is 2 days.
    • The count is for the whole area at once. Ten days in Spain and twenty in Germany are 30 days, not two separate allowances.
    • Days spent on a residence permit or a long-stay visa are not counted. If you lived in a Schengen country on a national digital nomad visa and then travelled as a tourist, only the tourist days go into the calculator.

    The European Commission's own calculator does the same count, and its user manual adds one handy fact: 90 days in a row outside the area always gives you a fresh 90.

    A worked example from the EU manual

    The manual, which we opened on 6 November 2026, follows a visa holder through 2024. The stays were 1–10 January (10 days), 1–30 March (30 days) and 1 May – 9 June (40 days). That is 80 days. Here is what the person could do next, by the date of entry:

    Entry date Longest stay that fits Why
    19 June 2024 20 days 10 days are left, and the January days drop out of the window one by one from 29 June
    7 August 2024 20 days, leave by 26 August January is out, March is still in until 27 August
    8 August 2024 50 days March drops out day by day from 28 August, so each new day inside replaces an old one
    8 September 2024 90 days 90 days in a row outside since 10 June

A second example in the manual, a visa-free traveller who came for one day on 1 January 2024 and returned on 1 June, reaches 90 days on 29 August 2024: that was the last allowed day, and staying on until 20 September was an overstay.

    Which countries count: the Schengen area on 6 November 2026

    The EU's Your Europe page and the Commission's Schengen page, both read on 6 November 2026, list 29 countries. Bulgaria and Romania were the last to join, on 1 January 2025, so days there count from that date.

    Group Countries
    25 EU countries Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden
    4 non-EU countries Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, Switzerland
    Not counted Cyprus and Ireland (EU, but outside the Schengen rules), the United Kingdom

    The Commission says Cyprus is still being brought into the area and Ireland has an opt-out. Ireland and the UK have their own entry rules; for the UK see what a UK ETA costs and covers.

    When the calculator's answer is not your answer

    The count is the general rule. Four cases change it, and the EU manual names each of them:

    1. Your visa sticker allows fewer than 90 days. Then the sticker is the limit, and the stay must also fit within the visa's validity dates. The Commission's calculator page says not to use the calculator in that case.
    2. Your country has an old bilateral visa waiver agreement with one Schengen state. Under Article 20(2) of the Schengen Convention some states let, for example, Canadian, New Zealand or US citizens stay extra time in that state on top of the 90 days. Whether a state still applies it is up to that state; no calculator can know.
    3. You hold an ordinary Brazilian passport. The EU–Brazil agreement keeps the old wording, three months during six months from the first entry, so the manual does not recommend the calculator.
    4. You hold a long-stay visa or a residence permit. Those days are outside the count altogether.

    Your passport has its own deadline as well: for a short stay it must be valid at least 3 months after the day you plan to leave. Our page on passport validity rules by country has the details.

    How to fill in the calculator

    1. Take the dates from your passport stamps, tickets or boarding passes. Put each trip in its own row.
    2. If you are inside the area right now, leave the exit date of the last stay empty. The calculator treats it as running to the check date.
    3. Set the check date. It is today unless you change it, and you can move it to any past or future day.
    4. For a trip you are planning, add its entry date and, if you know it, its length. You get the longest stay from that date and, if your trip does not fit, the first date when it would.

    The border guard decides

    The Commission's calculator page and its manual both say the tool only helps: it gives no right to stay for the period it shows, and the decision belongs to the border guards and other authorities of the Schengen states. Treat our number the same way. It is a careful estimate of the general rule.

    If the count is close to 90, or your case is one of the four above, check with the consulate of the country you are going to. If you want the official fact for your passport and destination, describe your dates to the NeedPro bot: it answers from its checked set and shows the source.

    Questions people ask

    Do the day of arrival and the day of departure count?

    Yes. Article 6(2) of the Schengen Borders Code says the date of entry is the first day of stay and the date of exit is the last. A trip from Monday to Friday is 5 days, and a same-day visit is 1.

    Does the 180-day period start on my first entry?

    No. The period moves: on each day of your stay you look back 180 days from that day. The old rule of 3 months in the 6 months after first entry is gone, except for ordinary Brazilian passports, where the EU manual says the old wording still applies under the EU–Brazil agreement.

    How long do I have to stay out to get 90 days again?

    The EU user manual says an absence of 90 days in a row always allows a new stay of up to 90 days. You can often come back sooner for a shorter trip; the calculator shows the earliest date for the trip length you enter.

    Do days in Ireland, Cyprus or the UK count towards the 90?

    No. Ireland and Cyprus are in the EU but not in the Schengen area for this rule, and the UK is outside the EU. Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland are outside the EU but inside Schengen, so their days do count.

    Can I stay longer than 90 days with a visa-free passport?

    Only under a separate permission. Some Schengen countries still apply old bilateral visa waiver agreements that allow extra time in that one country, and a long-stay visa or residence permit lets you stay longer. The calculator cannot see either; ask the consulate of that country.
